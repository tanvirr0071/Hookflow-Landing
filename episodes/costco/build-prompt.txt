Build a YouTube Short in Remotion from my voice-over: a "Markup" Short, "Costco's $1.50 Hot Dog Doesn't Need Profit".

THE GOAL
It must look like a premium cinematic animated documentary short: painterly, richly textured, deep, lit like a film, with character acting so natural it feels observed from real life. Character design keeps the clean appeal of a high-end anime feature, but every frame is rendered with volume, texture and atmosphere. It must never look like flat vector art, motion graphics, an explainer, a slideshow or a paper cut-out puppet. It plays as one continuous story that moves with the voice: the same world, the same characters and the same hero prop carry the viewer from shot to shot, and every shot hands off to the next. Every cut, every action and every piece of text is synced to the voice-over to the frame.

Work like a real feature-animation studio, in this order, and do not skip ahead:
timing → reference (motion and hands) → design (model sheets, hand sheet, props, texture kit, background plates) → layout and animatic → key poses → breakdowns and in-betweens → secondary motion → lighting, texture and compositing → QA.
Each stage has a quality gate (STEP 12). Do not start a stage until the previous gate passes.

PRIORITIES WHEN SOMETHING HAS TO GIVE
1. Sync and facts are never compromised.
2. Believable movement and hands. A character who moves with real weight in a simple frame beats a detailed character who moves like a paper puppet.
3. Quality over quantity. Fewer, better drawings, well staged, beat many crude ones. Use real animation staging cheats freely: silhouettes against bright windows, backs of heads, over-the-shoulder framing, characters cropped by foreground objects, and moving holds.
4. Every frame must survive a pause. If a paused frame looks like a vector explainer, a PowerPoint slide, clip art or a cut-out puppet, it fails.

STEP 1 — TIMING MAP (do this first)
1. Get the exact audio duration with getAudioDurationInSeconds from @remotion/media-utils and confirm it with ffprobe (they must agree within one frame).
2. Composition "Costco": 1080x1920 at 30 fps. durationInFrames = Math.ceil(duration * 30) + 4. The last 4 frames are the loop hold (see the last shot).
3. Transcribe: make a 16 kHz mono WAV copy of the voice-over in a temp folder (whisper.cpp requires it). Install whisper.cpp and the large-v3-turbo model with @remotion/install-whisper-cpp and transcribe with tokenLevelTimestamps: true; use the DTW token timestamps (t_dtw) where present. Merge sub-word tokens into words.
4. Align Whisper's words to the script below with a sequence alignment. The script wins: "Costco" may come out as "Cosco" or "Costcos", "Jelinek" as "Jellinek", "Jelenik" or "jelly neck", "Sinegal" as "Senegal", "Sinagal" or "Sinegol", "effing" as "f-ing", "effin'" or "freaking", "buck fifty" as "buck 50", and numbers may come out as digits or symbols ("$1.50", "1.50", "245 million", "80s", "$5.9 billion", "5.9", "2024", "9 in 10", "US", "$65"); map them to the script's words. Save src/data/costco/words.json as [{ word, scriptIndex, startMs, endMs, rawFrame, frame }].
5. Onset refinement: Whisper word starts are often 40–120 ms off. Decode the voice-over to PCM, compute a 10 ms RMS envelope, and snap every cut word and beat word to the nearest speech onset within ±3 frames. Run ffmpeg silencedetect (noise=-35dB:d=0.18) and record every pause. Keep both the raw and the refined frame.
6. Build src/data/costco/timeline.ts: for every shot its cut word, cut frame, end frame (the next shot's cut frame) and every beat word inside it with its refined frame, plus helpers such as beat("s05", "word"). All animation reads from this file. Never hard-code seconds or absolute frames in components; offsets relative to a beat (beat - 3) are fine.
7. Cut rule: a shot cuts in on its CUT word (usually the first word of the line). Actions land on their BEAT words. A big hit never lands on a shot's first frame: leave at least 6 frames between the cut and the first big hit so the anticipation reads.
8. Sync rule: every action tied to a word lands ON that word's refined start frame. Big actions get 2–4 frames of anticipation before the hit (a wind-up, a squash, a held breath), then follow-through and settle over 6–12 frames after it.
9. Pace: a new shot or a clear new action every 2–3 seconds. If a shot runs past 3 seconds on my voice, add a camera change or a new action on its next beat word. Something meaningful moves at least every second.
10. Write out/costco/timing-report.md (shot, cut word, word time, raw frame, refined frame, every beat word → frame, every pause with its length) and out/costco/sfx-cues.csv (timecode HH:MM:SS:FF at 30 fps, frame, shot, action, suggested sound: receipt printer chatter, paper tear, light-switch click, door jolt thud, rubber stamp thunk, adding-machine clatter, iron weight slam, wallet snap…) so I can add SFX by hand.

RENDERING STYLE (painterly and cinematic, not flat cel)
- Line art is thin, coloured and secondary to light: lines use the darkest local colour (never pure black), are heavier in shadow and on outer contours, thin out or break where light hits an edge, and taper at the ends (build them as filled shapes, not uniform strokes). Forms are defined mainly by light and shadow.
- A reusable <ShadingStack> renders every character part and prop in this order: base colour → form shadow with a soft terminator (2–6 px feathered edge; colour = base tinted by the scene's shadow colour, never grey) → a darker core shadow and ambient occlusion in creases (under the chin and hair, inside the elbow, between the fingers, where a body meets a seat) → on skin, a warm, slightly saturated band along the terminator → bounce light on undersides from the floor or nearby colours → highlight → rim light in the key light's colour → specular glints on eyes, hair, lips, nails, a watch and phone glass.
- Store the key-light direction per shot in style.ts; shadow and light shapes follow it, so a character who turns toward the light brightens on that side.
- Cast shadows: characters and props cast soft shadows onto floors, walls, desks and each other, matching the key light's direction and softness. Contact shadows sit wherever something touches a surface: the tray on the counter, feet on the polished concrete floor, the bookkeeper's elbows and the stamp on the desk, the balance's right pan on the floor.

TEXTURE (every surface, characters included)
- Build a texture kit once, procedurally and seeded (no outside images), with scripts/build-textures.ts, and pre-render it as seamless PNG tiles: paper grain, canvas weave, dry brush, wet-paint blooms, chalk dust, concrete, wet asphalt, skin micro-texture, brushed metal, wood grain, glass smudges, plus thermal paper (the receipt and the calculator tape), foil-lined paper (the hot dog sleeve), steamed bun crumb and glossy frank skin (the hero hot dog), red moulded plastic (the trays), chipped enamel over wood (the old sign), poly-cotton piqué (the polos), cotton drill (the bib apron), nitrile (the worker's gloves), waxed canvas (the member's jacket), fleece (his hoodie), wool knit (the bookkeeper's cardigan), engraved brass (the nameplates, plates, picture lights and balance), mahogany veneer and frosted glass (the corridor door), green plaster (the corridor wall), insulated steel panels (the plants), cast iron (the vault weight), carbonless form paper (the deposit slip), PVC card stock (the member cards), worn leather (the wallet), polished concrete (the warehouse floor). Reuse tiles that earlier episodes already built.
- On characters, each clothing and skin area gets its matching tile at low opacity (multiply or soft light), clipped to its shape, plus a brush mask that breaks up shadow edges so they look painted, not vector-perfect. Fabric shows seams, stitching, buttons, pockets, buckles, knit patterns and wrinkles that change with the pose.
- In backgrounds, brushwork follows the form, edges break up, shadows hold painted texture, surfaces show dirt and wear, and fine detail concentrates around the focal point and falls away from it, as in a painting.
- Texture must be visible in a 100% crop and subtle at phone size.

DEPTH (every shot)
- Combine depth cues: the real perspective camera (STEP 5), overlap, scale change, atmospheric haze between layers, depth of field with bokeh, cast and contact shadows, light falloff, and a foreground element close to the lens in most shots.
- Plan the value structure in depth for each shot in the layout: the subject at the focal plane gets the highest contrast; the foreground is darker or out of focus; the background is hazier.
- Volume: thin fog cards between depth layers; visible light beams (skylight shafts over the food court, the dusk shaft through the corridor's end window, headlight beams and sodium lamp cones in the mist at the plants, the spotlight cone over the museum case, the banker's lamp cone in the cash office, morning shafts through the entrance doors); steam from the hero hot dog and the steam well, dust motes, paint flecks from the old sign, paper fibres from torn tape, and vent steam and mist at the plants catching the light.
- Bokeh: out-of-focus point lights (register screens and card terminals, the high-bay lamps, sodium lamps and truck lights at the plants, the CCTV monitor, the parking-lot lamps) become soft discs with a slightly brighter rim, sized by their distance from the focal plane.

PAINTED BACKGROUNDS
- No flat fills anywhere. Every surface has a gradient, brush texture (feTurbulence into feDisplacementMap on edges, low-opacity noise multiplied over fills) and hand-placed detail: cables, signage, window frames, scuffs, plants, reflections, light pools.
- Atmospheric perspective: far layers have lower contrast and are tinted toward the sky or ambient colour.
- One key light, one fill, and practical lights (neon, screens, lamps) casting coloured pools on nearby surfaces.
- Background plates: render each background layer once as a high-resolution transparent PNG through its own composition (Plate-s01-far, Plate-s01-mid…) with `npx remotion still`, saved to public/plates/costco/. Plates are rendered at 2× pixel density (`--scale=2`) and 1.25–2× the frame size on the axis the camera travels, so texture stays crisp through push-ins. Shots then composite plates + live elements (characters, lights, text, particles). This lets the painting use expensive filters without slowing the render. Keep the plate source code so plates can be re-rendered.

RIG (skinned, never a paper cut-out)
- Skeleton: pelvis → lower spine → chest (a 3-segment spine), neck, head; clavicles (the shoulders rise when an arm lifts above shoulder height); upper arm, forearm with twist, wrist; thigh, shin, foot, toes.
- Skinned body: never rotate rigid SVG pieces around pins. Compute each limb's outline every frame from its bones with a width profile (deltoid, upper arm, forearm taper, wrist; thigh, knee, calf, ankle), so elbows and knees bend smoothly: the inner side compresses and creases, the outer side sharpens into the joint. The torso outline follows the three spine bones the same way, so the chest and waist bend and twist. No seams, gaps or overlapping circles at joints, at any angle.
- Two-bone IK with pole vectors for arms and legs: hands are placed on their targets (the red tray, the taped strip, the register key, the receipt tape and the tear bar, the light switch, the deposit bag's loops, the mop handle, the museum case's glass, the till drawer, the deposit slip, the rubber stamp and the ink pad, the envelope of fee slips, the marker and the jar, the calculator keys, the calculator tape and its masking-tape flag, the hoist release lever, the renewal notice, the membership card, the cards and the self-inking stamp, the wallet) and feet are planted; a foot carrying weight never slides.
- 2.5D face rig: the eyes, brows, nose, mouth, ears and hairline sit on a virtual ellipsoid and move with head yaw and pitch. During a turn the far eye narrows and moves toward the edge, the nose moves toward the silhouette, the cheek and jaw contour change, the ear shifts, and the hair clumps layer over and under accordingly. For turns beyond about 45°, swap to a dedicated 3/4 or profile head at the fastest point of the turn, where the motion hides the swap.
- Torso turns work the same way: lapels, buttons, belts and seams slide across the torso shape.
- Pose library per character, stored as joint angles + IK targets + face values + hand poses in src/data/costco/poses/.
- Every part draws its own shading stack and texture (STEP 2) from the shot's key-light direction.

HANDS (treat them as their own sub-project)
- Proportions: the hand is about as long as the face from chin to hairline, and the palm about as long as the middle finger. The middle finger is longest; index and ring are about equal; the pinky's tip reaches the last knuckle of the ring finger. The knuckles form an arc, not a straight line. The thumb starts at the wrist, its tip reaches about the middle knuckle of the index finger, and it sits at roughly 90° to the palm (it opposes the fingers; it never lies flat beside them).
- Hand rig: wrist, palm, thumb (3 joints: base, middle, tip) and four fingers with 3 joints each. Each finger is one continuous skinned shape built from its bones with a width profile: slight bulges at the knuckles, a narrower middle, a rounded, padded tip, tapering toward the end.
- Detail: the back of the hand shows nails (correct shape, a highlight, a cuticle line), knuckle creases and wrinkles, and on older characters tendons, veins and softer, older skin; the palm shows the thumb's padded base, the main creases and fingertip pads. Hands get the same shading stack, texture, cast shadows and contact shadows as the rest of the body.
- Views: back, palm, thumb side, pinky side and 3/4. Choose the view per pose so the hand reads clearly; never show a hand edge-on where the fingers merge into one shape.
- Curl rules: when fingers curl, the joints fold progressively (base least, tip most), curled fingers converge toward a point near the base of the palm, and each finger toward the pinky curls slightly more than the one before. Relaxed hands are never flat: the fingers rest in a gentle cascade of curls.
- Grips are layered: the object sits between the hand's layers (palm and far fingers behind it, near fingers and thumb in front), the fingers flatten slightly where they press, and contact shadows sit where skin meets the object. Tray: the fingertips curled under the near rim, the thumb on top of the rim, the other fingers fanned under the base; in a two-hand scoop, both thumbs on top. Receipt and calculator tape: pinched between the thumb and the side of the index finger, the other fingers curled, pulled down across the tear bar. Deposit bag: the fingers hooked through the handle loops, the thumb resting on the outside. Mop handle: a loose overhand grip, the other hand cupping the top. Till drawer: both hands under its ends, the fingers curled under the edge, the thumbs on top. Rubber stamp: the wooden knob in a full fist, the thumb wrapped over the top, the wrist straight for the slam. Envelope: pinched at its open end between the thumb and fingers, tipped from the wrist. Marker: pinched between thumb and index, the middle finger supporting it. Hoist release: four fingers hooked over the lever, the thumb under it, pulled down from the elbow. Self-inking stamp: the palm on top of the handle, the fingers wrapped round the frame. Membership card: pinched between the thumb pad and the side of the index finger, the thumb pushing it into the slot. Wallet: held open in the other hand, the fingers behind the fold, the thumb holding the flap. Renewal notice: a flat palm on top, the fingertips pressing as it slides.
- Motion: the wrist leads and the fingers follow 1–3 frames later; when a hand opens or closes, the fingers move in a cascade from index to pinky, never all at once. Fingers never pop between poses; they interpolate through the rig.
- Pose library (from my reference photos when available, see STEP 3B): relaxed, point, flat palm slap, pinch-and-tear, light-switch flick, fingers hooked through a loop, two-hand tray scoop, knuckle tap, mop lean, stamp fist, envelope tip, marker pinch, one-finger key punch, flag slap, lever yank, flat palm slide, card pinch with thumb push, wallet hold, and the worker's gloved hand resting on the tray rim in frame 0 (her hands are in blue nitrile gloves in shots 1, 2, 17 and 20: the same rig and anatomy, with glove wrinkles at the knuckles and a sheen).
- Close-ups (shots 1, 2, 12, 13, 14, 18 and 20, and any insert) get the full detail level; wide shots simplify detail but keep correct proportions and silhouettes.

ACTING (observed from life, never puppet-like)
- Nobody is ever perfectly still: breathing moves the chest, shoulders and clavicles (1–2 px, a 3–4 second cycle, a different phase per character), and standing characters make tiny weight shifts between their feet. A held character is a moving hold.
- Eyes lead: in every look or turn, the eyes move first, the head follows 2–4 frames later, then the torso. While watching or thinking, the eyes make small darts (saccades) every 0.5–1.5 seconds. Blinks take 5–7 frames (a fast close, a slower open), come every 2–4 seconds (seeded) and often accompany a head turn or a change of thought.
- Asymmetry: no mirrored poses. Weight sits on one leg with the hips and shoulders tilted in opposite directions; the two hands do different things; the two sides of the face are never identical.
- Faces act in small amounts: brow tension, lid height, the corners of the mouth, a jaw shift. A flicker of the coming emotion appears a few frames before the main expression (a flash of doubt in the eyes before a smile drops).
- Extreme close-ups on the eyes for dramatic beats.
- Mouths change only for expressions (smile, laugh, open surprise, flat). Characters never lip-sync: the narration is a voice-over; they act and react.
- Eyelines: characters look at what matters (the menu board and its taped strip, the receipt, the nameplates, the wall lettering, the red lettering on the glass, the tagged hot dog, the museum case, the deposit slip, the jar, the calculator tape, the tray and the vault weight, the renewal notice, the hanging sign, the stacked cards, the wallet), and screen direction stays consistent across cuts.

FRAME RATE
Characters animate on ones (every frame, 30 per second) for smooth, natural movement; the puppet look is fixed with posing, timing, skinning and overlap, not by dropping frames. Camera, particles, lights, text and effects are also on ones.

STEP 5 — CAMERA, LAYOUT AND ANIMATIC
- Build a reusable <Camera3D>: a real perspective camera in CSS 3D (perspective on the root, transform-style: preserve-3d), with every plate, character and prop placed at its true depth (translateZ, scaled to keep its intended framing size). The camera has a position (x, y, z), rotation, a lens (think 24 mm wide, 35 mm, 50 mm, 85 mm portrait) and a focus distance. Push-ins are dollies (the camera moves, so near layers grow faster than far ones), not zooms. Depth of field: each layer's blur comes from its distance to the focus plane and the shot's aperture; rack focus eases over 8–16 frames with a hint of focus breathing (1–2% scale). Handheld drift from noise2D in @remotion/noise (2–4 px, at most 0.3°), seeded per shot, with a documentary-camera feel: slow and organic on handheld shots, almost none on tripod shots.
- Depth layers: 5–8 in every exterior, 4+ in every interior, and in most shots a foreground element near the lens that crosses the frame (a paper soda cup and a napkin dispenser on the counter, the edges of red umbrellas, the hanging sign's chains, door frames and pilasters, the chain-link fence at the plants, a velvet-rope stanchion, the lamp's pull chain, the curling calculator tape, cart handles and flatbed carts passing the lens).
- Every shot has a camera plan: start framing, end framing, move type, easing and the beats that trigger changes. No locked-off camera; a deliberate held beat is still a moving hold (drift + atmosphere).
- Screen direction: progress and time move left to right; the bookkeeper walks the corridor left to right and the corridor whip-pans follow his head turns (right to the wall, left back to the door); the truck rolls left to right past the plants; the balance's heavy pan drops on the right and the tray flies up on the left; members stream into the warehouse left to right; the final whip-pan runs left and up, back to the menu board, the return to frame 0; a whip-pan continues the direction of the motion that motivated it.
- Layout first: write out/costco/layouts.md with one block per shot: framing (wide, medium, close, extreme close), camera move, layers, character key poses with their beat words, every text with its surface, the transition in and out, and the object that hands off to the next shot.
- Animatic: render a fast pass with grey placeholder shapes in the correct positions and the real timing (`--scale=0.25`), watch it against the voice and fix the timing here, before painting and animating.

STEP 6 — MOTION LANGUAGE
- src/lib/ease.ts holds named curves (anticipate, overshoot, settle, slam, whip, float) and a few named spring() configs. Linear motion is allowed only for mechanical constant speed: the receipt and calculator tape feeds, the refrigerated truck rolling past the plants, the glass entrance doors sliding open.
- Every move follows anticipation → action → overshoot → settle.
- Breakdowns: between every two key poses, add a breakdown pose that defines the arc and the overlap (never a straight interpolation from key to key). Spacing is slow out of a pose, fast through the middle and slow into the next, and no two body parts start or stop on the same frame.
- Biomechanics: the centre of gravity stays over the supporting foot; before reaching, pulling or lifting, the body shifts its weight first; to lift, the knees bend and the back stays straight; effort shows in the whole body (shoulders tense, neck strains, a short pause before a heavy lift).
- Timing from life at 30 fps (refine from reference): blink 5–7 frames; eye dart 2–3; head turn 8–14; reach for an object 12–20; tap 4–6; both arms up 10–16; hand opening or closing 6–10; sit-to-stand 30–45; the palm slap on the strip: 4 frames of wind-up onto her toes, 2 of action, 8–10 of settle; the tape tear: 3 frames of tension, 2 of rip, 8 of flutter; the knuckle tap on the glass: 4–5; the stamp slam: 6–8 frames of rocking on the ink pad and lifting, 2 of drop, 10–12 of lift and settle; the two-hand tray scoop and hurry-off: 6 of reach, 4 of lift, 12–16 of turn into the walk; the hoist yank and weight drop: 6 frames of reach, 3 of yank, 8–10 of a slow-then-fast fall, 2 of impact, 12–16 of the beam rebounding and settling; the card into the wallet: 6 frames of lining up, 3 of slide, 6 of snap and fold.
- Secondary motion is simulated: bake the hanging sign and its chains, the price tag on its string, the taped strip's loose corner, the receipt and calculator tapes, the worker's apron ties, the bookkeeper's glasses cord, cardigan hem and deposit bag, the member's jacket hem and hoodie strings, the banner, the lamp's pull chain, the balance's pans and chains with a deterministic spring/verlet simulation in scripts/bake-secondary.ts; save per-frame values to src/data/costco/sim/ and read them in the components. Remotion renders frames in parallel, so anything physical must be baked or computed purely from the frame number.
- Weight: heavy things (the cast-iron vault weight, the double-sided hanging sign on its chains, the heavy corridor door, the vinyl banner, the rubber stamps, the till drawer) start slowly, stop hard and cause a small shake; light things (the receipt and calculator tape, the taped paper strip, the price tag on its string, the fee slips, paint flecks and steam) float, tumble and flutter.
- Arcs: hands, heads and props travel on curved paths (IK targets follow arcs); never slide a hand in a straight line.
- Overlapping action: the head leads, the torso follows 2 frames later, arms 2–4 frames later, hair, hems, bags and loose clothing 2–4 frames after that.
- Smear frames (1–2 frames) on fast moves: the palm slap on the strip, the tape tears, the door jolt, the bookkeeper's tray scoop, the stamp slams, the vault weight's drop and the tray flying up, the card snapping into the wallet.
- Impact frames (2–3 frames, restrained and filmic: an exposure flash and contrast punch with a touch of speed lines and very slight chromatic aberration) on key hits only, so they keep their punch: "profit" (shot 1), "effing" (shot 7), "billion" (shot 12, the stamp), "billion" (shot 15, the vault weight), "renew" (shot 18).
- Screen shake on big hits: decaying over 3–6 frames, maximum 8 px.
- Motion blur with @remotion/motion-blur during fast moves only (whip-pans, the door jolt, the stamp slams, the vault weight's drop and the tray flying up, the truck's headlights sweeping past the lens), with a 180° shutter feel and a low sample count to protect render time.
- Particles (steam from the hero hot dog and the plant stacks, dust motes, paint flecks from the old sign, paper fibres from the tears, ink spatter from the stamp, mist at the plants, fee slips tumbling into the jar) are deterministic and layered in depth: near ones are bigger, blurrier and faster. Each piece tumbles in 3D (scaleX = cos of its spin) and drifts on its own curve.

STEP 7 — KINETIC TYPOGRAPHY INSIDE THE WORLD
- No captions, no word-by-word subtitles and no floating text cards. Every text sits on a surface in the world.
- <SurfaceText> places text on any four-corner quad using a perspective transform (matrix3d computed from the four corner points), so it sits exactly on its sign, screen, paper or board and moves with that surface's parallax layer.
- Light the text by surface type: emissive (screens, LED, neon: glow and bloom, and it lights nearby surfaces), printed (multiplied by the scene light, paper texture, shadowed), handwritten (marker or chalk texture), stamped.
- Occlusion: foreground objects pass in front of text when natural (the falling receipt tape passes in front of the old sign's lettering in shot 3; the bookkeeper's soft shoulder crosses the red lettering on the glass in shots 7–8; the lamp's pull chain swings across the deposit slip in shot 12; members and carts pass in front of the "RENEW" decal in shot 19).
- Entrances land on the exact beat frame and match the surface: LED digits roll and land; neon flickers on (2–3 flickers within 6 frames); screens switch on with a one-frame bright pop; printed text arrives with its object; handwriting is written stroke by stroke; stamps slam with a squash. Exits are motivated: switched off, wiped, washed out, torn, fallen away, scrolled off.
- Handwriting: in a Node script, convert the needed glyphs of Permanent Marker (the .woff from @fontsource/permanent-marker) to outlines with opentype.js and save them to src/data/costco/glyphs.json. Reveal each glyph in writing order through a mask of thick strokes that follow the glyph's centre line, animated with evolvePath from @remotion/paths; the marker or chalk tip follows the leading point (getPointAtLength). Writing finishes on its beat word. If a glyph's centre line is not available, reveal it with a directional wipe along its main stroke, still followed by the pen.
- Fonts via @remotion/google-fonts: "Anton" for the menu board, the factory banner, the roof signs, the billboard and the door decal; "IBM Plex Mono" for the receipt, the calculator tape, the deposit slip header, the tray tag, the renewal notice and the member cards; "Cormorant Garamond" for the brass nameplates, the wall lettering, the attribution plates, the price tag and the museum plaque; "Alfa Slab One" for the old painted sign, the red lettering on the glass, the stamped lines, the jar label and the lettering cast into the vault weight; "Permanent Marker" for the taped strip, the fill line on the jar and the masking-tape flag. Check that every special character (“ ” ’ · ≈ × → —) exists in the font of its surface; if one is missing, move that surface to another of these fonts that has it, and if Permanent Marker has no ≈, draw it as two stacked marker tildes through the same stroke pipeline. Load only the weights you use, and confirm in the stills that no fallback font appears. Fit long lines with fitText from @remotion/layout-utils.
- Readability on a phone: every key text and key action stays inside x 48–888, y 288–1248 of the 1080×1920 frame (clear of the Shorts buttons and title). Main words have a cap height of at least 72 px; secondary text (small print, attributions, source tags, sticky notes) at least 34 px and on screen for at least 1.2 seconds. Every text has strong contrast with what is behind it. Background art can fill the whole frame.
- Add a showSafeZone prop that draws the safe box, for QA stills only.

STEP 8 — TRANSITIONS
- Lay the shots out on an absolute timeline (<Sequence from={cutFrame}> with premounting), and build each transition as an overlay that straddles the cut, so the audio timeline never shifts. If you use TransitionSeries from @remotion/transitions, remember that transitions overlap the shots and shorten the total: compensate the durations so every cut still lands exactly on its cut frame, and verify it.
- Allowed transitions, each motivated by the story:
  - Whip-pan (6–8 frames): accelerate out with motion blur and speed lines, cut at maximum blur on the cut frame, decelerate in, continuing the screen direction.
  - Light-flash cut: a bright source in shot A blooms toward white over 3–4 frames; cut; shot B blooms down from its own brightest light over 4–6 frames.
  - Match cut: a shape or motion in shot A matches the position, scale and direction of one in shot B on the exact cut frame.
  - Continuous camera move: the camera travels from one setup to the next without a cut.
  - Foreground wipe: an object passes the lens, fills the frame and the cut happens behind it.
- Never crossfades, dips to black or generic slides.

STEP 10 — COMPOSITING AND FINISH
- Layer order in every shot: plates → haze cards between depth layers → practical light pools → cast and contact shadows → characters (shading stack, texture, rim light, light wrap) → in-world text → atmosphere (particles) → light beams → glow, bloom and halation on emissive surfaces → lens flares → grade → grain → vignette → chromatic aberration (impact frames only).
- Bloom: a blurred copy of emissive layers composited with a screen blend.
- Skylight shafts over the food court: tall, cool, soft-edged columns with dust and steam drifting in them; the dusk shaft through the corridor's end window, raking amber across the panelling; headlight beams and sodium lamp cones cutting the mist at the plants; the hard spotlight cone over the museum case, with dust hanging almost still in it; the warm cone of the banker's lamp over the cash office desk.
- Screen glow on faces: a coloured soft-light shape on the side of the face toward the screen that follows the screen's brightness.
- Lens flares on the menu board's lit panel, the truck headlights, the museum spotlight, the billboard floodlights and the morning sun through the entrance doors.
- Film finish in a reusable <Grade> (per-scene settings in style.ts): a filmic contrast curve with slightly lifted blacks and a soft highlight roll-off; split toning (shadows toward the scene's ambient colour, highlights toward the key light); halation (a thin warm-red glow around the brightest highlights and practical lights); light wrap (bright backgrounds bleed a few pixels over character edges); very subtle gate weave (at most 0.5 px).
- Grain: reuse the earlier grain component, changing every frame, stronger in mid-tones and shadows than in highlights, at 4–7% opacity; pre-render a set of grain tiles and cycle them if per-frame turbulence slows the render. A gentle lens vignette (10–15%) with slight edge softness.
- Keep renders fast: no full-frame SVG filters every frame; prefer pre-rendered plates, transforms and opacity.
